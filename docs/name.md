# The name

<p align="center"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/x-ryoppippi/dupin/main/assets/logo/dupin.svg" alt="Dupin's mark: a pair of green spectacles" width="128" height="128" /></p>

Dupin is named after [C. Auguste Dupin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._Auguste_Dupin), [Edgar Allan Poe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Allan_Poe)'s detective. Mac search tools and launchers have a line of names that runs Sherlock → Watson → Alfred: a detective, his companion, a butler. Dupin is Alfred's successor, and the line continues in it. It takes its name from the detective at the line's source: Dupin came before [Sherlock Holmes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherlock_Holmes), and Holmes's author owed the detective story to Poe.

## The detective at the source

Poe wrote three stories about C. Auguste Dupin, an amateur who solves crimes in Paris by reasoning, told by an unnamed friend who shares his rooms:

- "[The Murders in the Rue Morgue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Murders_in_the_Rue_Morgue)", in _Graham's Magazine_, April 1841.
- "[The Mystery of Marie Rogêt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mystery_of_Marie_Rog%C3%AAt)", in three parts in _Snowden's Ladies' Companion_, November and December 1842 and February 1843.
- "[The Purloined Letter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Purloined_Letter)", in _The Gift_ for 1845, published in 1844.

Dupin is generally acknowledged as the first detective in fiction; E. T. A. Hoffmann's _Das Fräulein von Scuderi_ (1819) is sometimes cited as earlier.

[Arthur Conan Doyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Conan_Doyle) acknowledged the debt. In [_A Study in Scarlet_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Study_in_Scarlet) (1887), the first Holmes story, Watson tells Holmes he reminds him of Poe's Dupin, and Holmes answers that Dupin "was a very inferior fellow". Doyle didn't share his detective's opinion. Presiding at the Poe centenary dinner in London on 1 March 1909, he asked: "Where was the detective story until Poe breathed the breath of life into it?"

### The names

What the makers say about their names is less than the line suggests. Here is what is documented, apart from Dupin's author's reading of it:

- **Holmes from Dupin: documented.** Conan Doyle named Poe's Dupin as where the detective story began, and had Holmes and Watson discuss him in the first Holmes story.
- **Sherlock from Holmes: in the name only.** Apple's own words, as quoted, say only that Find File "has been expanded and renamed Sherlock"; no statement by Apple on the choice of name has been found. The reference is in the name itself and in the icon, a detective's hat with a magnifying glass.
- **Watson to Sherlock: documented.** Karelia's FAQ says Watson was "envisioned as Sherlock's 'companion' application", and its product page punned on Holmes ("Making the Web Elementary!"). Dan Wood's reason for the name itself isn't recorded.
- **Alfred as a butler: the common reading, not documented.** Running with Crayons hasn't explained the name; its early site called the logo a butler's hat, and the press read the name as a butler's. Peter Maurer, whose launcher is called [Butler](https://manytricks.com/butler/), saw Alfred as a tribute to it, while noting the makers couldn't have known that he had privately called Butler "Alfred", after an uncle. Butler itself was renamed from Another Launcher because the new name better represented what it did, its release notes say.
- **Sherlock → Watson → Alfred → Dupin: Dupin's author's reading.** Read in order, the names run from a detective to his companion to a butler. Dupin goes back past all of them to the detective the line starts from, and carries Alfred's way of working forward.

### Sherlock

Apple's search tool for the Mac bore Holmes's name. [Sherlock](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherlock_(software)>) shipped with Mac OS 8.5 in 1998, an extension of the Finder's own search that looked for files and searched the web from one window. Sherlock 2 came with [Mac OS 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mac_OS_9) in 1999 and introduced channels.

Sherlock 3 came with [Mac OS X 10.2 Jaguar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mac_OS_X_Jaguar), released on 24 August 2002, and turned the channels into small applications built on web services: Pictures, Stocks, Movies, Yellow Pages, eBay, Flights, Dictionary, Translation and AppleCare among them.

Mac OS X 10.4 Tiger (April 2005) moved searching for files to Spotlight. Sherlock still shipped with Tiger, though most of Apple's own channels had stopped working, and Mac OS X 10.5 Leopard (2007) left it out.

### Watson, and "Sherlocked"

[Karelia Software](https://www.karelia.com/)'s [Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karelia_Watson), written by Dan Wood and released in November 2001, took Sherlock 2's channels further in a Mac OS X app: each of its tools fetched one kind of information from a web service and showed it in its own native view. It won the 2002 Apple Design Award for the most innovative Mac OS X product.

Months later Sherlock 3 did much the same. Ars Technica's Jaguar review called it "a dead-ringer for Watson", though the two had no formal relationship; Apple's view was that Sherlock 3 was the natural next step from Sherlock 2. In 2004 Watson's technology went to Sun Microsystems (Karelia says Sun bought it; Wikipedia says Sun licensed it), and Karelia stopped developing the Mac app and made it free.

The episode gave Mac developers a word. When Apple builds into its own software what a third-party app already does, the app has been _Sherlocked_.

### LaunchBar

Sherlock and Watson searched; launchers find the thing you want on your own Mac and act on it from the keyboard. [LaunchBar](https://www.obdev.at/products/launchbar/index.html), by Objective Development, began on NeXTSTEP: the idea dates from 1995 and the first public version from 1996. Its authors say they chose Command-Space as its hotkey in 1995. It came to Mac OS X in 2001 as LaunchBar 3. You type a short abbreviation, and LaunchBar learns which item you mean by it: the abbreviation you used to pick something becomes a stronger match for it next time.

### Quicksilver

[Quicksilver](https://qsapp.com/), written by Nicholas Jitkoff at Blacktree from 2003, made a grammar of it. Its window has three panes: an object, an action and an optional second object, like the subject, verb and object of a sentence. You pick a file, then an action such as sending it by email, then whom to send it to. Triggers bind such a command to a hotkey, and plug-ins add objects and actions. Jitkoff opened its source in 2007, under the Apache License 2.0, and a [community](https://github.com/quicksilver/Quicksilver) has maintained it since.

### Butler and Quick Search Box

Peter Maurer's [Butler](https://manytricks.com/butler/), first called Another Launcher, was a launcher of the same era, by 2004; it is still sold by Many Tricks. Jitkoff moved to Google, where he and Dave MacLachlan released [Quick Search Box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Quick_Search_Box) for the Mac in January 2009, an open-source launcher in Quicksilver's manner. Its last build is from 2010, and it was left unfinished.

### Alfred

[Alfred](https://www.alfredapp.com/), by Andrew and Vero Pepperrell's Running with Crayons, came out in February 2010, a launcher with a butler's name and a bowler hat for its icon. Short keywords summon everything from it: `in` searches inside files, `define` a word, a web search's keyword the web. It learns from what you pick: it ranks what you use often higher, and latches the exact query you typed to the result you chose, so that query shows it first. The paid Powerpack adds clipboard history, snippets and, since Alfred 2 in 2013, workflows, which shared workflows in the [Alfred Gallery](https://alfred.app/) extend.

### Spotlight

[Spotlight](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spotlight_(Apple)>) came with Mac OS X 10.4 Tiger in 2005 as file search in the menu bar, and grew into a launcher: Command-Space opens it today. OS X 10.10 Yosemite (2014) moved it to a window in the middle of the screen with results from Wikipedia, Maps and the web. macOS 26 Tahoe (2025) gave it actions from apps, quick keys and clipboard history, features that third-party launchers had offered for years.

### Raycast

[Raycast](https://www.raycast.com/), founded in 2020 by Thomas Paul Mann and Petr Nikolaev, opened a public beta that October. Its [extensions](https://developers.raycast.com/) are programs written in TypeScript with React that run on Node and draw their own views; since November 2021 they have been published through the Raycast Store, whose extensions live in [one public GitHub repository](https://github.com/raycast/extensions).

## Why Dupin was built

Dupin's author wanted a light, resident launcher in Alfred's manner, and the line it continues is Alfred's, not Raycast's. To its author, Raycast has become a small window for running little programs more than a launcher. Alfred's short keywords that summon all sorts of things are what the author valued most, but Alfred felt heavy to them, its development and its shared workflows seemed to have slowed, and its workflows are assembled in Alfred's Workflow Editor, a canvas on which you place objects and connect them. A workflow made that way isn't something an agent can write as text, which doesn't fit how the author makes software now.

Another reason was smaller. [Tinycast](https://github.com/abue-ammar/tinycast), a native launcher written in Swift, was a candidate, but its author declined Alfred's Space on an empty query to search files ([#1070](https://github.com/abue-ammar/tinycast/issues/1070), closed as not planned: Space types a space, and file search is reached through a fallback or its own shortcut). A later request for Space or Tab after an alias to enter its command was closed as a duplicate of requests already turned down ([#1185](https://github.com/abue-ammar/tinycast/issues/1185)).

So Dupin is keyword-first, as Alfred is: Space on an empty query searches files, and a keyword and Space enters a command ([Using Dupin](./usage.md)). It stays resident in the background with no Dock icon. Its [extensions](./features/extensions.md) are plain TypeScript or JavaScript files with their manifest in `package.json`, so an agent can write one, test it with `dupin ext test` and `dupin ext snap` ([Testing and snapshots](./features/extensions.md#testing-and-snapshots)), and install it from Git ([Installing and updating](./features/extensions.md#installing-and-updating)). Each runs sandboxed and reaches only what you allow it, and there is no store.

## The logo

Holmes is drawn with one period object, a deerstalker or a magnifying glass, and Alfred's icon is a bowler hat. Dupin's object is the pair of green spectacles he puts on in "The Purloined Letter" to call on the Minister and look over his room without being seen to. Dupin's mark is those spectacles, abstracted: two green lenses joined by a bridge, on a dark tile ([`assets/logo`](../assets/logo)).

## What Dupin carries forward

- **Learning from what you pick.** As LaunchBar and Alfred do, Dupin remembers which result you opened for which query, so the same query puts it first among matches as good as it, and a shorter query that begins a longer one you used ranks it higher too. A pick counts less as it ages, so an old habit gives way to a new one. Beside that it keeps frecency, how often you opened something weighted towards recently, with the age buckets Firefox's address bar uses. Both live in [`history.rs`](../crates/dupin-core/src/history.rs), and neither ever lifts a scattered match over a query typed from the start of a word.
- **Kinds and actions.** Quicksilver's object and action survive as Dupin's kinds: every row is text, a URL, a file or an app, and its kind gives it actions. Cmd+K opens a file's [actions](./features/file-actions.md), such as Open with…, Move to… and Send to…, and [`[advanced]`](./features/advanced.md#kind-actions) adds your own actions to every row of a kind, run by a script, a URL or an extension.
- **Extensions in TypeScript, without a store.** As Raycast's are, Dupin's extensions are TypeScript packages, but Dupin draws their views. They run in QuickJS-ng in a sandboxed helper process, not Node, and reach nothing on their own: each declares the capabilities it wants, such as one website or a folder you pick, and you allow each one, and can revoke it ([Asking for more](./features/extensions.md#asking-for-more)). Packages are installed from Git and pinned by commit, and `dupin ext search` lists GitHub repositories with the `dupin-extension` topic, marked unreviewed, because the broker, not a review, protects you ([design](./design/extension-platform.md#packaging-and-distribution)).
- **Your data stays yours.** There is no account. What Dupin keeps, such as usage history, clipboard history, snippets and extensions' storage, is [encrypted on disk](./features/storage.md) under a key kept in your Keychain. [Sync](./features/sync.md) between Macs goes through a folder you already sync, such as iCloud Drive, end-to-end encrypted, with no Dupin server; today it carries usage history, and settings, snippets and the rest are to follow.
- **Keywords and aliases.** As in Alfred, a short [keyword](./features/keywords.md) and Space enters a command (`cal`, `clip`, `define`), and `g` or `gh` starts a [web search](./features/web-searches.md). [`[aliases]`](./features/keywords.md#aliases) lets you add your own shorthands for a command, an app, a web search or a file.
